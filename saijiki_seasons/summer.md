# Summer

## The Season

### Summer

夏 natsu

Summer is commonly viewed as the season with the longest, warmest, and sunniest days, roughly spanning June, July, and August. According the traditional Japanese calendar, summer is officially marked as the time between the solar terms of "start of summer" (立夏 rikka) and "beginning of autumn" (立秋 risshu), roughly the fourth through the sixth month on the lunar calendar. According to the the solar calendar, summer begins midway between the vernal equinox and the summer solstice and the seasons ends midway between the summer solstice and the autumnal equinox. Due to variable seasonal lag, the meteorological center of the season, the hottest day based on average daily temperature, normally falls several weeks after the summer solstice.

The poetic essence of summer is that it is the hottest of the seasons and the time when the days are the longest and the nights the shortest.  The season is divided into three parts: early summer- characterized by new, light green leaves; midsummer- the dreary rainy season; and late summer- the time of the hottest days of summer.

### 06. Start of summer

Beginning of summer

立夏 rikka

"Beginning of summer" is the seventh of the twenty-four solar terms that make up the traditional Japanese year. In the premodern Japanese lunar calendar, “start of summer”  begins early in the fourth month. On the Gregorian calendar, this period generally starts on the fifth of May and ends around around May 21. While this period marks the official start of summer in Japan, in the northern part of the country cherry blossom are finally coming into full bloom at this time and even in the southern regions the sultry weather is yet to begin.

### Shallow summer (slight)

夏浅し natsu asashi

The early part of the season, when according to the calendar summer has begun, but the weather is still mild is described as "shallow summer."  During this time of year, the spring blossoms have scattered, but the tree leaves are yet to completely sprout and their color is still light.

### 夏めくnatsu meku

Summery or summer-like

After the blossoms of spring have scattered the trees leaves grow luxuriant and everything in nature looks and feels like summer. This change from spring to summer can be felt in the daily lives of people: in house furnishings, clothing, and in the types of food people eat. The season word "summery" is closely related to “shallow summer,” but the latter focuses more on the fresh greenness of nature, while the former suggest the summer-like appearance of people.

### Early summer

初夏 shoka

In the first part of summer a special freshness can be felt in the wind and sunlight. All throughout nature, in the feathery clouds, the color of lakes and the ocean, and in the grass and flowers now covering the fields, the buoyant expansiveness of summer is most clearly felt at this time of year.  Whereas "start of summer" refers to the time of the calendar year, "early summer" suggests the weather.

### Wheat's autumn

麦の秋 mugi no aki

In early summer the landscape is adorned with golden ripened wheat.  While most grains ripen in autumn, wheat ripens in summer, so this time is called "wheat's autumn."  Under the bright, clear blue sky dotted with white clouds, farmers are busy harvesting and preparing for the next year's crop.  The kanji characters can also be read as "bakushu," which is the name for the fourth lunar month.  This was not a popular topic in classical waka, where there are only a few poems mentioning the autumn wind in wheat (mugi no aki kaze), but toward the end of the middle ages it began to be used in renga and came to be widely used in haiku.

### 夏の日　natsu no hi

Summer day or summer sun

The character "日" can mean both "day" and "sun," and in this season word is used both ways.  Early summer days may be comfortable, but as the season advances into the humid, rainy period, the sun grows more intense and the days become hot and sultry.  In summer the sun is so clear and hot that it stands out much more than during any of the other seasons.

### Summer nights

夏の夜 natsu no yo

In the summer nights are short, but after the long hot day people often go out to enjoy the cool air.  In the evening, people go outside to sit or walk about, and children play with fireworks.  As it is difficult to sleep in the heat, people stay up late into the night.  In The Pillow Book Sei Shonagon designated the night as the best part of the season saying, "In summer the nights are delightful.  When there is a moon this is obvious, but on dark nights as well, for many fireflies flit about."

Variations: Summer midnight (夜半の夏 yowa no natsu)

### Short nights

短夜 mijika yo

The nights of summer are the shortest nights of the year.  The days gradually lengthen after the spring solstice, until at the height of summer.  While this season word is closely related to “summer nights,” there are subtle differences.  “Summer nights” conveys more of an easy going feel of enjoying the cool of the night.  “Short nights,” on the other hand, express a feeling of regret at the evanescence of the night during summer.  Many classical poems explore this topic, most of which use the phrase “quick to dawn” (明けやすし ake yasushi).

### 鹿の子 Kanoko — Fawn [Summer Dobutsu]

The young of deer which mate in autumn; it is born between mid-April and June. Chestnut brown with white spots (kanoko madara), the young male has a bump on the head. Horns grow out in its second year. Fawns are gentle and shy, but generally friendly to people. It is sweet to see them following close by their mothers as though trying to gain attention.

Variations: shika no ko (the deer’s child), kojika (baby deer), mejika (female deer).

### 蝙蝠 Komori — Bat [Summer Dobutsu]

The bat or flitter-mouse is the only mammal that flies. Coming out of hibernation in spring, in the daytime it hangs upside down under eaves or in trees, and can be seen flying about from dusk and during the night, in search of insects. As it is fond of eating mosquitoes, old names were kakui dori (a bird that eats mosquitoes) and kawahori (a bird that wants mosquitoes). There are varieties that feed on fruit trees. The bat was not a theme in poetry, but the sight of them flying on a misty summer evening has its own special taste and interest for haiku.

**Poems:**

1. かはぼりや -- むかひの女房 -- こちを見る -- 蕪村
   Kawahori ya / mukai no nyobo / kochi wo miru  —  Buson
   Bats — / the wife across the street / looks this way.
   They may both have been watching the bats on this warm evening and by chance, their eyes met in a charming moment.

2. かはぼりや -- 月のほとりを -- 立ちさらず -- 暁台
   Kawahori ya / tsuki no hotori wo / tachi sarazu  —  Gyodai
   Bats — / they don’t leave the vicinity / of the moon.

3. 我が宿に -- 一夜たのむぞ -- 蚊喰鳥 -- 一茶
   Waga yado ni / hitoyo tanomu zo / kakuidori  —  Issa
   To my house, / please come a night! / Bird that eats mosquitoes.

### 雨蛙 Amagaeru — Rain Frog [Summer Dobutsu]

This species of frog, the toad and the river frog are summer topics. The rain frog is about 4 cm long, and wakes from winter sleep in early April. It lives on tree boughs and leaves, hence is also called ada-gaeru (branch frog). Ordinarily it is green, but it has the ability to change colour to match the environment, turning brown on reaching the ground. When rain is impending or has begun to fall, it cries in a loud voice, unlike its size. Ao-gaeru (green frog), a slightly different species which also inhabits trees, is included in this kigo.

**Poems:**

1. 雨蛙 -- 芭蕉にのりて -- そよぎけり -- 其角

   A rain frog / got onto a plantain leaf, / and swayed.

2. 麦藁の -- 家してやらん -- 雨蛙 -- 智月
   Mugewara no / ie shite yaran / amagaeru  —  Chigetsu
   Let me make you / a wheat-straw house, / oh rain frog.

3. 鳴きて知りぬ -- 石蕗の若葉の -- 雨蛙 -- 素丸

### 蟇 Hikigaeru — Toad [Summer Dobutsu]

This is the largest of the frogs, with a dark, yellow-brown back full of warts. It comes out of hibernation in February, lays eggs, goes back to sleep and crawls out again in early summer. The toad can grow to 12 cm or more; its warts emit a white, sticky, poisonous fluid. In the daytime it stays in damp shades, thick grasses, or holes in the ground. Around dusk it saunters out in its dull and deliberate way to catch small insects and earthworms, and sits without moving, as though in meditation. With its grotesque yet sagacious and mysterious appearance, the toad is a creature that has the taste of hai.

Variations: hiki, gama or gamogaeru.

**Poems:**

### 河鹿 Kajika — River Frog [Summer Dobutsu]

A small, thin variety of frog that lives in mountain lakes and streams. It is brown, with some dark grey spots. Around the time new leaves sprout on trees, the male, sitting on a rock by a riverside, starts to sing in a pure, clear voice, “hyorohyoro hihihi.” As people of old admired the deer’s voice, they called this frog “river deer.” The word “kawazu” used in many poems of the Manyoshu seems, from the context, to be referring to this river frog, and some which say “kajika” and “kawazu” seem to mean the ordinary frog. In the Edo period, kajika was confused with a fish of the same name, miller’s thumb, an autumn kigo.

### 時鳥 Hototogisu — Little Cuckoo [Summer Dobutsu]

Like spring’s flowers, the autumn moon and winter snow, the little cuckoo has been considered to be the important theme characteristic of summer. It comes to Japan from the south around May and lives in the mountains till the end of autumn. Its feathers are dark grey-brown; the breast and underside are white with stripes running across. It has the peculiar habit of depositing its eggs in the nest of the bush warbler. People of old were eager to hear this bird, waited for its first song, and once heard, wished to hear it again. That song is spirited and haunting, “teppenkaketaka”, “honzonkaketaka”, or the like.

There are many legends about the little cuckoo from China. Some said it was the spirit of the ancient emperor Wang of Shuh; others, that it was a bird that came and went between here and hell. They referred to it as the bird of impermanence, the bird that spits blood, or the field elder. Hototogisu was written with many different kanji.

**Poems:**

1. はつ声を -- 聞かまほしきに -- 時鳥
   *Hatsu koe wo / kikama hoshiki ni / hototogisu*
   > Wishing to hear / your first song, / little cuckoo —

2. 夜深く -- 目をも -- さましつるかな
   *Yo fukaku me wo mo / samashitsuru kana*
   > How late I’ve stayed / up till, tonight!  —  Poet unknown, Tai waka collection

3. ほととぎす -- 大竹藪を -- 漏る月夜 -- 芭蕉
   *Hototogisu / o takeyabu wo / moru tsukiyo  —  Basho*
   > Little cuckoo — / through a large bamboo grove, / the moonnight.

4. ほととぎす -- 鳴いて行方や -- 島一つ -- 芭蕉
   *Hototogisu / naite yukue ya / shima hitotsu  —  Basho*
   > Little cuckoo — / in the direction it faces, / an island.

5. ほととぎす -- 鳴くや五月の -- 雨の中 -- 其角
   *Hototogisu / naku ya gogatsu no / ame no naka  —  Kikaku*

   > Little cuckoo — / from an unexpected direction, / the first song.

### 老鶯 Oi uguisu — Old Bush Warbler [Summer Dobutsu]

While the bush warbler is a spring topic, oi-uguisu means the bush warbler singing in summer, its breeding period. At this time it returns to mountainous areas, bamboo groves and forests to make its nest. Hence, it is only in such places that it can be heard. Because uguisu has always been related to spring, the summer uguisu is called in haiku, “old” or “remaining” uguisu.

Variations: natsu uguisu (summer bush warbler), ro-o (old bush warbler), miyama uguisu (deep-mountain bush warbler), zan-o (remaining bush warbler).

**Poems:**

1. 鶯や -- 竹の子藪に -- 老を鳴く -- 芭蕉
   Uguisu ya / take no ko yabu ni / oi wo naku  —  Basho
   The bush warbler — / in a grove of bamboo shoots, / sings of old age.

2. 老鶯や -- 青嶺 -- 折々 -- 雲の上 -- 徳永冬子
   Ro-o ya / seirei oriori / kumo no ue  —  Tokunaga Santeshi
   An old warbler — / green peaks sometimes appear / above the clouds.

3. 老鶯や -- 珠のごとくに -- 一湖あり -- 富安風生
   Ro-o ya / tama no gotoku ni / ikke ari  —  Tomiyasu Fusei

4. 老鶯や -- 出湯のあまりが -- 谷に落つ -- 秋元不死男
   Ro-o ya / deyu no amari ga / tani ni otsu  —  Akimoto Fujio

### 葦切 Yoshikiri — Reed Warbler [Summer Dobutsu]

Of the family of warblers, category of sparrows, there are the Great reed warbler (oyoshikiri) and the Black-browed reed warbler (ko yoshikiri). The former is more common; it resembles the bush warbler but is slightly larger, growing to about 18 cm in body length. It is light green-brown with a yellow-white belly. It crosses to Japan from the south in May, to live in flat areas with swamps, by lakes and watery ricefields, where reeds grow, until September. Its name (reed-cutter) is because of its skill at splitting a stalk of reed to get the insects in it. Another name, gyogyoshi, is after its song, which is loud and noisy in the hot weather. The Black-browed reed warbler’s voice is finer and clearer.

Variations: gyogyoshi, yoshi-suzume, ashi-suzume (reed sparrows).

**Poems:**

1. 能なしの -- 寝むたし我を -- ぎやうぎやうし -- 芭蕉
   No nashi no / nemutashi ware wo / gyogyoshi  —  Basho
   To sleepy me, / with no abilities, it sings — / reed warbler.

2. 芦原の -- 中に家あり -- 行々子 -- 正岡子規
   Ashihara no / naka ni ie ari / gyogyoshi  —  Masaoka Shiki
   In the middle / of a reed plain, a house — / reed warblers.

3. 信濃川は -- 分流多し -- 行々子 -- 大谷句仏
   Shinanogawa wa / bunryu oshi / gyogyoshi  —  Otani Kubutsu
   Shinano river / has many tributaries — / reed warblers.

4. 葦切のをちの鋭声 -- 朝曇り -- 水原秋桜子
   Yoshikiri no / ochi no eisei / asa gumori  —  Mizuhara Shuoshi
   A reed warbler’s / distant, shrill voice — / morning cloudiness.

### 翡翠 Kawasemi — Kingfisher [Summer Dobutsu]

A beautiful bird with green feathers like nephrite, chestnut brown on the underside, and red feet. It lives in green shades by gorges and swamps. Perched on a stake or tree top with eyes fixed on the water, when it spies a fish it will fly in a straight line to catch it with its large beak. It is a resident bird and may be seen in all seasons, but its colours and agility of movements invite coolness, so that it was made a kigo of summer. Other names are hisui, amagoi dori (bird that begs for rain), mizugoi dori (bird over lower water).

**Poems:**

1. かはせみや -- 絵の具を流す -- おのが影 -- 馬光
   Kawasemi ya / enogu wo nagasu / onoga kage  —  Bako
   The kingfisher — / making watercolours flow, / his reflection.

2. かはせみや -- 羽をよそほふ -- 水鏡 -- 露川
   Kawasemi ya / hane wo yosohou / mizu kagami  —  Rosen
   The kingfisher — / it dresses its feathers / in a water mirror.

3. 翡翠や -- ぬれ羽にうつる -- 夕日影 -- 桃李
   Kawasemi ya / nureba ni utsuru / yu hikage  —  Tori

4. 翡翠 -- とぶ -- その四五秒の -- 天地かな -- 加藤秋邨
   Hisui tobu / sono shigo byo no / tenchi kana  —  Kato Shuuson
   A kingfisher flies — / those four, five seconds / of a world.

### 水鶏 Kuina — Water-Rail [Summer Dobutsu]

A small bird resembling a snipe, the water rail (also known as muddy crake) comes to Japan in spring from the south, and returns in the autumn. It is red-chestnut colour from head to breast, while the rest of the body is dark olive. It walks in the thick growth of reeds by watersides in lowlands, stops once in a while and says “hobkyokyokyo”, which sounds like knocking on a door. From the early times of imperial collections of poetry, this bird has been connected with visiting or awaiting someone, or with the realization of a short night.

**Poems:**

1. 水鶏啼くと -- 人へいへばや -- 佐屋泊り -- 芭蕉
   Kuina naku to / hito no ie baya / Saya domari  —  Basho
   “A water-rail sings,” / as someone said, / stop at Saya.
   Although he hadn’t planned to stop at Saya, because a disciple invited him, saying he could hear a water-rail, he did. Saya is in Amagun, Aichi prefecture.

2. 馬洗ふ -- 川すそ闇き -- 水鶏かな -- 北枝
   Uma arau / kawa suso kuraki / kuina kana  —  Hokushi
   Washing the horse / at a dark river-mouth, / a water-rail.

3. 聞くうちに -- すゑまぼろしの -- 水鶏かな -- 青羅
   Kiku uchi ni / sue maboroshi no / kuina kana  —  Seira
   While listening, / it ended like a phantom — / the water-rail.

4. 水鶏啼いて -- 星うく草の -- はやまかな -- 澄翠
   Kuina naite / hoshi uku kusa no / hayama kana  —  Chosui
   A water-rail calls, / and stars float — / grassy hill.

### 鮎 Ayu — Sweet-Fish [Summer Dobutsu]

From ancient times this fish has been prized as the king of river fish. Ayu hatch in autumn rivers and grow up in the warm gulfs of winter. In early spring they go upstream as young ayu (waka ayu). They are about 20–30 cm long in summer and have a noble appearance; their backs are olive green, bellies are white with a streak of yellow. People fishing for ayu with poles in pure streams is a summery sight. Weirs and cormorants are also used.

Variations: ayu no yado (sweet-fish inn), kogyo (perfumed fish), nengyo (fish that lives a year).

**Poems:**

1. 釣竿に -- 鮎のあはれや -- 水はなれ -- 北枝

3. 鮎釣の腰を真白に -- 水たぎつ -- 木津柳芽

4. 上流は曲りて見えず -- 鮎の川 -- 山口波津女

### 金魚 Kingyo — Goldfish [Summer Dobutsu]

The miniature species of carp bred for ornamental purposes. They were imported from China in the 16th century to enjoy their variety of beautiful forms, and have since been cross-bred in ponds. There are now many kinds, of different shades of red, gold and silver with spreading tails or bulging eyes — such as Ranchu, Ryukin, o-naga, Demekin, to name a few. To see them swimming through green duckweed in a glass bowl enhances coolness. The goldfish pedlar with his tubs and goldfish bowls calling out his wares, used to be a regular part of the summer scene. At present they are mainly at shrines, where goldfish can be scooped from their trays with nets or ladles.

Variations: kingyo-uri (goldfish pedlar), kingyo dama (goldfish bowl), kingyo sukui (scooping goldfish).

**Poems:**

1. 底を行く -- 金魚に動く -- 真砂かな -- 岡本松浜
   Soko wo yuku / kingyo ni ugoku / masago kana  —  Okamoto Shohin
   Going along the bottom, / the goldfish stirs / the white sand.

2. 生涯の -- 今の心や -- 金魚見る -- 高浜虚子
   Shogai no / ima no kokoro ya / kingyo miru  —  Takahama Kyoshi
   Of a lifetime, / the heart of now; / look at goldfish.

3. 女たち -- おしゃべり -- 金魚 -- 浮き沈み -- 山口青邨

4. しだり尾の -- 錦ぞ動く -- 金魚かな -- 河東碧梧桐

### 初鰹 Hatsugatsuo — First Bonito

The bonito is a hard-boned fish of the mackerel family. Schools of bonito ride on a black current and approach the south eastern shores of Japan around the time when the trees are filled with the green leaves of summer. Then, fishing begins, and the first batches that are caught are called the first bonito. They were immediately sent from Odawara and Kamakura to Edo, where, being highly prized, they were vied for. Bonito grow to more than 50 cm in length, have few scales and firm, red flesh. They are eaten raw, or dried and shaved into flakes and used on vegetables or as soup stock.

**Poems:**

1. 目には青葉山郭公初鰹 -- 素堂

   > the mountain little cuckoo,
   > and first bonito.

2. 鎌倉を生きて出でけん　初鰹 -- 芭蕉
   *Kamakura wo / ikite ideken / hatsugatsuo -- Basho*

   > left Kamakura alive,
   > the first bonito.

   *Kurenai wa / hana ni kagiraji / hatsugatsuo -- Ryota*

   > not limited to flowers —
   > the first bonito.

4. 初鰹　聞きおよんだる　ばかりなり -- 岡野知十

### 蝉 Semi — Cicada [Summer / Dobutsu]

The shrill song of this insect is an inevitable part of the summer landscape. In the mountains, cicadas begin to sing around May, but after the rainy season ends in July, many kinds of cicadas can be heard — the abura zemi (oil cicada) that goes "jijiji", the minmin semi that sings its name, kuma zemi (bear cicada) that says "shaa shaa", the niinii zemi that also sings its name. Heard in the afternoon, cicada voices sound hot, but in the mornings or dusks, they can sound cool.

They are mostly dark brown with transparent wings. Only the males sing; the females, being silent, are called oshi zemi (dumb cicadas). In comparison with the long period spent in the earth, the life of the cicada is very short, so that it was frequently used allegorically as a motif of impermanence by poets.

Variations: hatsu-semi (first cicada), semi-shigure (a shower of cicada voices).

**Poems:**

1. 頓て死ぬ　けしきは見えず　蝉の声 -- 芭蕉
   *Yagate shinu / keshiki wa miezu / semi no koe -- Basho*
   > Soon to die,
   > it does not seem —
   > the cicada's voice.

2. 閑さや　岩にしみ入る　蝉の声 -- 芭蕉
   *Shizukasa ya / iwa ni shimi iru / semi no koe -- Basho*
   > The stillness —
   > into boulders sink
   > cicada voices.

3. 蝉鳴いて　杉にも脂の　流れけり -- 関更
   *Semi naite / sugi ni mo yani no / nagare keri -- Ranko*
   > Cicadas singing,
   > even from cedars,
   > resin flowed.

4. 唖蝉も　鳴きをはりたる　さまをせり -- 加藤楸邨
   *Oshi zemi mo / naki owari taru / sama wo seri -- Kato Shuson*

### 蚊 Ka — Mosquito [Summer / Dobutsu]

### 蟻 Ari — Ants [Summer / Dobutsu]

The sight of ants busily coming and going in rows through gardens or paths is indeed a part of summer. These are working ants, active in warm weather, carrying supplies for the winter for their well-organized society headed by the queen ant. When ants become mature, they have wings; in this state they are winged ants (ha-ari). After mating, the wings drop off and the males die gradually, while the females return underground to lay eggs.

Variations: yama ari (mountain ants), ari no michi (road of ants), ari no to (ant hill), ari no retsu (row of ants).

**Poems:**

1. 蟻の道　雲の峰より　つづきけん -- 蕉
   *Ari no michi / kumo no mine yori / tsuzukiken -- Joso (attr.)*
   > A road of ants —
   > from a cloud peak,
   > continuing.

2. 塵取にすこしかかりて　蟻の道 -- 大橋桜坡子
   *Chiritori ni / sukoshi kakarite / ari no michi -- Ohashi Ohashi*
   > On the dust-pan

### 蛞蝓 Namekuji — Slug [Summer / Dobutsu]

A mollusc like a snail, without a shell, found along ditches, waterways and sinks. Its grey-brown body, about 6 cm, is covered with mucous membranes. It crawls by stretching and shrinking the stomach area, and leaves a silver slime behind it. Being fond of damp places, slugs can be seen during the rainy season or on rainy days. They feed on cabbages, fruit and leaves.

### 蝸牛 Katatsumuri — Snail [Summer / Dobutsu]

The snail reveals itself most frequently from around May to July, crawling slowly along the leaves of grasses, on tree branches or on stone fences. It carries a spiral shell on its back, in which it takes shelter in times of danger. Like the slug, it leaves a sticky, silver slime in its traces as it crawls slowly and deliberately, but unlike it, the snail is rather fondled by children, who call it dendenmushi or dedemushi. It moves its horns apart as it crawls. Looking on its silver slime as written letters is the working of the mind of "kai." Also called katatsaburi.

   > the snail.

   > from the world.

### 牡丹 Botan — Peony [Summer / Shokubutsu]

This plant was brought to Japan about a thousand years ago from China, where it was known as the "king of the hundred flowers." As the Tang poet Po Chui wrote, "the flowers open, and fall, in twenty days," another name is the twenty-days flower. He compared it to the face of a beautiful woman.

The peony bush grows to a height of a meter or more; the large flowers can have a diameter of about 20 cm, and come in red-purple, white, pink, red, black-purple, and yellow, with a deep perfume. As they are so luxuriant, they earned the name fiki-gusa (grass of prosperity). Like the chrysanthemum and herbaceous peony (shakuyaku), the peony has long been a favorite subject of poets as well as painters. Places famous for peonies are Hase-dera temple in Nara, Taima-dera temple in Iwashiro, Sukagawa in Fukushima, to name a few.

Variations: shiro or haku botan (white peony).

**Poems:**

   > one on another, two,
   > three petals.

   > the peony.

4. 白牡丹　崩れんとして　二日見る -- 成美

### 薔薇 Bara — Rose [Summer / Shokubutsu]

The roses that came to Japan from China were called sobi (the Chinese reading of these kanji), and this name was used in the poetry of the 9th century. One strongly fragrant, rich flower blooms at the top of a branch covered with thorns. Although there are countless varieties, single and double, and those that bloom in spring and autumn, roses were fixed as a theme of early summer. In haiku, ibara, the wild field rose has been considered more important.

### 石楠花 Shakunage — Rhododendron [Summer / Shokubutsu]

An evergreen bush of the azalea family, native to the Himalayas, the rhododendron grows in fairly high mountains and valleys of Japan, to the height of three meters. The leaves are long, oval, with a leather-like texture, the front sides are smooth, the undersides have a brownish fuzz. In early summer five to eight-petaled flowers resembling the azalea, bloom in clusters at the tips of the boughs. The many varieties, pink, red, pale yellow and white, are greatly admired and planted in gardens.

Muroji temple in Nara, Kurokami mountain in Nikko, and Mt. Koya in Wakayama are a few places famous for rhododendrons.

### 花橘 Hana tachibana — Citrus Flower [Summer / Shokubutsu]

This is the fragrant, small 5-petaled white flower of the only wild variety of citrus that grows in warm areas near the seacoasts of Kyushu, Shikoku, Wakayama and central Japan. It blooms in June, the flowers more favoured than the inedible fruit. It is said that this evergreen tree was brought from the world of Eternity in ancient times, by Tajima Mori, and was known as tokijiku no konomi (the timeless perfumed fruit). In the 9th century it was planted in Kyoto's Shishinden palace grounds to represent the imperial guards of the right, while the cherry tree was set to represent those of the left. Considered to be a symbol of long life and eternity, old poets often referred to it as the home of the little cuckoo.

### 紫陽花 Ajisai — Hydrangea [Summer / Shokubutsu]

### 木下闇 Ko no shita yami — Under-Trees Darkness [Summer / Shokubutsu]

The shady darkness in daytime under thickly growing summer trees. It comes as a great relief as one enters a grove from the hot sunlight. As a kigo this psychological feeling may be said to be stronger than the physical. In old poetry it was referred to as ko no kure (the dusk of trees); but from Heian times the present expression, kono shita yami, came to be used and has continued through renga and haikai to the present. ### 若葉・青葉 Wakaba, Aoba — Young Leaves / Green Leaves [Summer / Shokubutsu]

Wakaba means the fresh, watery new leaves of various trees that make the landscape of early summer so refreshing and beautiful. The names of trees can be used, as in persimmon young leaves, oak young leaves, camphor young leaves. In 16th century renga it became a theme of summer and made distinct from kusa wakaba (young leaves of grasses), a topic of spring.

Aoba are the deeper green leaves after the wakaba stage, when they are lighter in colour, and became a kigo in the early twentieth century. The expression aoba-wakaba, which was said in older haiku, refers to both light and dark green leaves.

Variations: mura wakaba (clusters of young leaves), wakaba kaze (wind in young leaves), wakaba ame (rain on young leaves), aoba wakaba (light and dark green leaves), aoba yama (mountain of green leaves).

   > Dark and light green leaves
   > in the sun's lustre.

2. 不二ひとつ　うづみ残して　わかばかな -- 蕪村
   *Fuji hitotsu / uzumi nokoshite / wakaba kana -- Buson*

   > young leaves.

3. 里の灯を　ふくみて　雨の若葉かな -- 蕉太

   > The village lamps
   > shine in them,
   > young leaves in the rain.

### 葵 Aoi — Hollyhock [Summer / Shokubutsu]

Plants with the name hollyhock are generally divided into two groups. The aoi written of in the old poems of the imperial court is the futa-ba aoi or aoi gusa, a wild variety with beautiful, glossy leaves. It was considered a sacred grass and was used for the gala in the festival of the Kamo shrine. This festival came to be called Aoi Festival. In Edo times the pair of leaves of aoi gusa became the family crest of the Tokugawa shogunate. On haiku, if the Aoi festival is mentioned, this grass is meant.

In the second group are hana or tachi aoi, prized for the large flowers, zeni aoi with small flowers resembling coins, and others which generally grow tall and have flowers of various colours.

### A Spring

泉  Izumi  [Summer / Chiri]

Pure water that wells out of the earth in fields, from gaps in the ground, or in mountainsides under thick growth, from between rocks. ### Pure Water

清水  Shimizu  [Summer / Chiri]

This is pure water that flows out from a spring, mainly between boulders. Shimizu and izumi were used interchangeably in the old days, but izumi (a spring) implies some quantity of water, whereas shimizu focuses on the clear and icy quality of the water. Among the many waka composed on shimizu from ancient times, the following is very well known:

道の辺に -- 清水流るる -- 柳かげ / しばしとてこそ -- 立ちとまりつれ

"At the roadside, pure water flows under a willow shade — let me stop a moment, I thought, but lingered on."  — Saigyo

Pure water is named according to the different places they well out from, such as iwa-shimizu (boulder pure water), yama shimizu (mountain pure water), koke shimizu (moss pure water). It was made a summer kigo because of its coolness in the heat of this season.

**Poems:**

1. さざれ蟹 -- 足這いのぼる -- 清水かな
   *Sazare gani / ashi hai noboru / shimizu kana芭蕉  Basho*

   > crawls up my foot —
   > pure water.

2. 源は氷か雪か -- 苔清水
   *Minamoto wa / kori ka yuki ka / koke shimizu太無  Taimu*

   > ice, or snow?
   > Moss pure water.

3. 其底に木葉 -- 散ふる -- 清水かな
   *Sono soko ni / konoha toshifuru / shimizu kana正岡子規  Masaoka Shiki*
   > At the bottom,
   > tree leaves spend the years —
   > pure water.

To change from winter to summer clothing. ### Blinds

簾  Sudare  [Summer / Jinji]

Blinds made of thin, split bamboo, reeds or glass crystal, used to shade the rooms from the strong summer sunlight. In Heian times the furnishings of the house were also changed on the day of changing to summer clothing, and blinds of fresh bamboo were hung under eaves, at windows or between rooms. They prevent being seen from outside, while allowing the wind to pass through. The shadow of a woman seen through the blinds, or the garden, the moon and outdoor scenery through the slits, have a cool and summery taste.

### Talcum Powder

### Windbell

Resembling a miniature temple bell, the windbell is an implement hung under the eaves or by a window to catch the wind and produce a cool sound on a hot summer day. It is made of metal, ceramic or glass, with a small clapper hanging from its center, to which a small poem card is attached. When there is a breeze, the poem card will flutter and cause the clapper to tinkle. Originally a Chinese invention, first mention of it appeared in books of the Kamakura period.

### Revolving Lamp

走馬灯  Somato  [Summer / Jinji]

"Running-horse lamp" is the literal translation of somato. Paper or cloth is pasted around a square or cylindrical frame to make the shade. Shapes of horses, butterflies, birds and so on are cut of black or coloured paper and pasted on a cylinder placed inside the frame. When a candle set in its center is lit, a wind-wheel on the top of the spindle causes the inner cylinder to turn round, so that the cut-out forms appear to be running. Somato became popular from Edo times. Also called mawari-toro.

### Insect Airing

虫干  Mushiboshi  [Summer / Jinji]

The spreading out of clothing, books, paintings and the like, in outdoor shades, to prevent moulding and damage by insects. For this purpose, a clear, dry day during the hottest period of summer (doyo) was chosen. It was from the old days a yearly event, when Shinto shrines and Buddhist temples aired their treasures.

Variations: mushi harui (shaking off insects), doyo boshi (hot-days airing), bakusho (airing books).

**Poems:**

   > where you can see mountains —
   > hot day's airing.

2. 虫干に -- 猫も干されて -- 居たりけり
   *Mushiboshi ni / neko mo hosarete / itari keri一茶  Issa*

   > the cat, too,
   > being aired.

3. 虫干や -- 蔵の虫入に -- 松の風

### Rice-Transplanting

田植  Taue  [Summer / Jinji]

From early June till the beginning of July, rice seedlings are taken from their beds and transplanted into watered ricefields. This has been an important activity for more than a thousand years, as rice is the staple in Japan. In the old days taue was a ceremonial event, and prayers were made for a good harvest. Rice-planting songs were sung as many women worked together.

Variations: taue uta (rice-planting song), taue-gasa (rice-planting hats).

**Poems:**

### Grass-Cutting

草刈  Kusa kari  [Summer / Jinji]

Grasses in fields, foothills and roadsides are cut with a sharp sickle to use as feed for horses and cows, and as fertilizer. This was mainly done by youth and women as early as possible in the morning while still cool. The cut grass is spread out to dry and gathered at dusk. With miscellaneous tiny flowers included, the fragrance is strong and summery. Kusa kari is a kigo of the early 20th century.

Variations: hoshi gusa (drying grasses, or hay), kusa kari me (grass-cutting maiden).

**Poems:**

### Fish Weir

魚簗  Yana  [Summer / Jinji]

An ancient form of fish net woven of thin bamboo to catch ascending and descending fish in a river. Stakes are driven into the rapids, the water dammed with sticks or bamboo, and the weir is placed. Ayu (sweet-fish), trout, salmon and eels are the main varieties caught, but sometimes crucians, carp and catfish may be trapped. "Yana" alone was determined to be a summer kigo because of the coolness of fish jumping on the slatted bamboo in a swift current.

Variations: yana utsu (to set up a weir), yana-mori (weir guardian).

**Poems:**

   > lights it and goes.

   *Yana-mori no / kage arau nari / yana shibuki堀口星眠  Horiguchi Seimin*

### To Seek Coolness

納涼  Suzumi  [Summer / Jinji]

To avoid the mid-summer heat which is difficult to endure, and find a cool place and a refreshing breeze, regardless of time or place. The most fitting attire for this relaxing relief from the heat is a yukata, and a round fan in hand. In Edo the Ryogoku bridge on the Sumida river, and in Kyoto the riverside of Shijo were popular places for cooling off.

### Fireworks

花火  Hanabi  [Summer / Jinji]

The various and numerous fireworks used for display at riversides on a summer evening. Different types of gunpowder are mixed and arranged in tubes; when lit, they shoot up into the sky and bloom like flowers in all colours, or flow like water. Originally an autumn kigo, it has been changed to summer in recent times, as it is cooling to see fireworks at night.

Variations: age hanabi (putting-up fireworks), to hanabi (distant fireworks), te hanabi (hand fireworks).

**Poems:**

1. 花火尽て -- 美人は酒に -- 身投げけむ

   > the beautiful lady,
   > sunk in drinking.

### Summer Thinness

夏痩  Natsu yase  [Summer / Jinji]

As the summer months in Japan bring high humidity and temperatures, many people are affected with a weakening of the digestive system, poor appetite and loss of weight. This condition is called natsu yase, or natsu make (yielding to summer). Although a few poems on this subject appeared in the Manyoshu collection, suggesting eating eels as a counter-measure, it was not a theme of poetry, but haikai has found it very appropriate.

### Fifth Month Fifth Day

端午  Tango  [Summer / Jinji]

The kanji mean "the first day of the horse," the original meaning. This changed to the horse day of the fifth month, then to the fifth day of the fifth month. In Heian times a mid-summer court banquet was held on this day. It was believed that the strong scents of the blue flag and mugwort swept away bad spirits. Later, when this day became the festival for boys, carp banners were put up.

Variations: ayame no sekku (annual observance of blue flags), ayame no hi (blue-flags day).

### Carp Banners

鯉幟  Koinobori  [Summer / Jinji]

Flags in the form of carps. They appeared in the mid-Edo period, at first made of paper, now mostly of cloth or vinyl. They are hoisted up on poles for Boys' Festival, the fifth day of the fifth month (now May). The carp, able to climb waterfalls, symbolized valour and eminence. Originally used by samurai families, carp banners gradually spread among the common populace.

Variations: gogatsu koi (fifth month carp), fuki-nagashi (streamers of different colours).

**Poems:**

### Festivals

祭  Matsuri  [Summer / Jinji]

Matsuri is the general term for summer festivals of Shinto shrines. Festivals are held to honor the gods and to pray for protection against illness, harmful insects, disasters of fire, wind and water. At the center of matsuri is a procession of the god's carriage (omikoshi), transported and hoisted high in the air by children or men, accompanied by the music of flute and drums.

Variations: natsu-matsuri (summer festival), kami-matsuri (the gods' festival), matsuri-bayashi (festival music), matsuri-bue (festival flute), matsuri-daiko (festival drum).

### Gion Festival

祇園会  Gione  [Summer / Jinji]

The yearly festival of the Yasaka shrine in the Gion district of Kyoto; it is one of the three great festivals in Japan. It goes on from July 1 to 31, but the high points are from the 13th, when children make a procession to Yasaka shrine, to the 17th when floats mounted with decorative halberds make a parade. The origin of this festival goes back to the 9th century.

Variations: Gion bayashi (Gion festival music), hoko-matsuri (halberd festival), hoko no chigo (child who rides on a decorated carriage).

### Summer Purification

夏越  Nagoshi  [Summer / Jinji]

A Shinto ceremony conducted on the last day of lunar sixth month to cleanse the body of impurities and offenses. The principal methods of purification were to set a large wreath of lalong grass on the shrine grounds, through which people passed (chino wa kuguri), and to use paper dolls. White paper was cut into doll shapes, one's name written on it, breath blown on it and the impurities of the body rubbed onto it. Then the paper doll was floated down a river.

Variations: natsu barae (summer purification), misogi (ablution), katashiro (paper-doll image), misogigawa (ablution river).

### Goldfish Bowl

A round, glass bowl in which goldfish are kept. It used to be hung by the eaves or placed at a window. With duckweed or moss placed inside, it was cooling to see in the heat of summer. Such old-fashioned bowls have of course been replaced by rectangular aquariums, but the round ones had more taste and showed the goldfish in a more interesting way. This was made a kigo in Meiji times.

### New Tea

新茶  Shincha  [Summer / Jinji]

Tea made from the new leaves of the tea plant, picked from late spring into early summer, around the 88th day after Spring Begins. The tender leaves are steamed in a steaming basket and scrupulously crumpled with the hands while roasting in a baking pan. The high aroma and especially fresh taste of shincha are highly prized. At this time, banners with "New tea available" float in the wind and the scent of tea fills the streets with the air of early summer.

### Cocoon

繭  Mayu  [Summer / Jinji]

In haiku, when just "mayu" is said, it means the cocoon of the spring silkworm. After the fourth shedding of skin, the silkworm becomes transparent and enters the stage of spinning a cocoon. It is put into a cocoon holder (mabushi) and in about seven days, completes a cocoon. The cocoons are round, oval or cylindrical in shape, and are basically white, but some are tinged with green, yellow or red. At present, due to the reforms in the fiber industry, silk culture has declined.

### Cormorant Fishing

鵜飼  Ukai  [Summer / Jinji]

The ancient method of fishing for ayu (sweet-fish) in rivers with trained cormorants. This is still done every year from around mid-May to mid-October. The main cormorant handler stands at the front of the small boat, with as many as twelve cormorants to handle through ropes tied round their necks. A ring, placed in the neck of the bird, prevents him from swallowing the fish. The Nagara river in Gifu is the most famous for ukai.

Variations: u kawa (cormorant river), ubune (cormorant boat), ukaibi (cormorant-fishing lights), usho (cormorant handler).

### Tub-Bath

行水  Gyozui  [Summer / Jinji]

A simple way of washing off the day's sweat on a summer evening. A tub is easily transported, so that it can be placed in one's garden, under the eaves, in the kitchen or bathroom, and warm water added. In Edo times the religious element disappeared, and gyozui became a part of the ordinary scene of the times, as painted in ukiyoe and humorously related in literature.

**Poems:**

1. 行水の -- 女にほれる -- 烏かな
   *Gyozui no / onna ni horeru / karasu kana高浜虚子  Takahama Kyoshi*

   > lady, he's fallen for —
   > Mr. Crow.

   *Gyozui ya / tsuki ni fukaruru / abara bone臼田亜浪  Usuda Rio*

   > in moonlight being blown,
   > the ribs.

3. 行水や -- 暮れしゆく -- 松の深みどり
   *Gyozui ya / kure yuku matsu no / fuka midori金尾梅の門  Kaneo Umenokado*
   > Tub-bath —
   > as dusk falls, the pine's
   > deep green.

### Sitting on the Veranda

端居  Hashi-i  [Summer / Jinji]

To sit out on the veranda and try to catch a cool breeze on a summer evening. In the traditional Japanese house, the veranda (engawa) faced the garden. One could sit there in a yukata with a round fan and relax, burning mosquito-repellent incense to ward off mosquitoes. It was a popular theme in haikai, but at present, with air-conditioners and western-style housing, it is another typically Japanese thing that has disappeared.

**Poems:**

1. 蚊やりして -- 師の坊を待つ -- 端居かな
   *Kayari shite / shino bo wo matsu / hashi-i kana太鳥  Tairo*
   > With mosquito punk,
   > I wait for a monk,
   > sitting on the veranda.

2. ひとりづつ -- 座を外しくる -- 端居かな
   *Hitori zutsu / za wo hazushi kuru / hashi-i kana苑子  Enshi*
   > One by one
   > they leave their seats, come
   > to sit on the veranda.

3. 端居より -- こぼれてをり -- 芝に坐し
   *Hashi-i yori / i koborete ori / shiba ni zashi後藤夜半  Goto Yahan*
   > From the veranda,
   > they spilled down,
   > sat on the turf.

4. 端居して -- 明日逢ふ人を -- 思ひをり
   *Hashi-i shite / asu au hito wo / omoi ori星野立子  Hoshino Tatsuko*
   > Sitting on the veranda,
   > I think of the person
   > I'll see tomorrow.

### Rice-Planting Women

早乙女  Saotome  [Summer / Jinji]

The ladies of all ages who took the rice seedlings from their beds and transplanted them into watered paddies. It was believed from ancient times that a god was present in the ricefield, and the rice-planting women were serving the god. They wore dark blue unlined garments, leggings and coverings for the back of hands, red sashes and tasuki to hold up the sleeves, new sedge hats and white cotton towels around their heads. As farming has been mechanized, saotome have become rare.

**Poems:**

1. さをとめや -- 汚れぬ顔は -- 朝ばかり
   *Saotome ya / yogorenu kao wa / asa bakari其角  Kikaku*
   > Rice-planting girls —
   > unsoiled faces, only
   > in the morning.

2. 早乙女の -- 祭りのやうに -- 揃ひ出る
   *Saotome no / matsuri no yo ni / soroi deru涼菟  Ryoto*
   > Rice-planting girls —
   > like a festival parade,
   > come out in a row.

3. 早乙女の -- 下りたつ -- あの田この田かな
   *Saotome no / oritatsu ano ta / kono ta kana太祇  Taigi*
   > Rice-planting girls
   > step down, stand in that
   > field and this.

4. 早乙女や -- 泥手にはさむ -- 額髪
   *Saotome ya / doro te ni hasamu / hitaigami村上鬼城  Murakami Kijo*
   > Rice-planting girl —

## Observances

### Entering the Peak

峯入  Mine iri  [Summer / Shukyo]

This means climbing the mountains of Yoshino in Nara Prefecture, and particularly, the highest peak of Mt. Omine (1915 meters). It is for the purpose of doing austere Buddhist practices, begun by the ascetic En no Gyoja (c.701). The monks who practise in sacred mountains are called yamabushi; they wear white garments and head bands or caps. At present this is done in June or July.

**Poems:**

1. 貝吹いて -- 雪踏み分けよ -- 花の跡
   *Kai fuite / yuki fumi wake yo / hana no ato青羅  Seira*
   > Blow the conch shell,
   > make way through snow!
   > After the flowers.

2. 峯入やおもへば深き -- 芳野山
   *Mine iri ya / omoeba fukaki / Yoshino yama白雄  Shirao*
   > Entering the peak —
   > when you think of it, Yoshino
   > mountain is deep.

3. 峯入や -- 鳥さへ知らぬ -- 花を今日
   *Mine iri ya / tori sae shiranu / hana wo kyo梅舎  Baisha*
   > Entering the peak —
   > today, flowers even birds
   > don't know of.

4. 峯入の -- 老いてそのこる -- 美男眉
   *Mine iri no / oite mo nokoru / biman mayyu及川貞  Oikawa Tei*
   > He enters the peak —
   > aging, but still
   > handsome eyebrows.

### The Buddha's Birthday

仏生会  Busshoe  [Spring / Shukyo]

A commemoration of Shakyamuni's day of birth, lunar fourth month 8th day. A statue of him pointing to heaven and earth is placed in a wooden basin under a small, open pavilion before a temple. Flowers of the season are picked to line its roof, and worshippers come to pour amacha (sweet tea brewed of leaves of the creeping hydrangea) over the statue. This was a ceremonial observance first established in the 7th century by Empress Suiko.

Variations: Hana-matsuri (festival of flowers), hana mido (flowery pavilion), Kanbutsu (bathing the Buddha).

**Poems:**

1. 灌仏の -- 皺手合はする -- 数珠の音
   *Kanbutsu no / shiwa te awasuru / juzu no oto芭蕉  Basho*
   > Bathed the Buddha —
   > wrinkled hands together,
   > rub the rosary.

2. 山寺や -- 五色にあまる -- 花み堂
   *Yamadera ya / goshiki ni amaru / hana mido蕉太  Ryota*
   > Mountain temple —
   > more than five colours,
   > the flowery pavilion.

3. 灌仏の -- 御指の先や -- 暮の月
   *Kanbutsu no / oyubi no saki ya / kure no tsuki一茶  Issa*
   > At the bathed
   > Buddha's august finger-tip,
   > the dusk moon.

4. 大空の -- 下あるき来て -- 花御堂
   *Ozora no / shita aruki kite / hana mido吉岡禅寺洞  Yoshioka Zenjido*
   > Walked under
   > the great sky — to the
   > flowery pavilion.
