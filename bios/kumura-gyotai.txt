Kyōtai (暁台, 1732–1792) was an Edo-period haiku poet born on October 19, 1732, in Nagoya in Owari Province. Born Kishinoue Nakahachi, the eldest son of an Owari domain samurai, he was adopted into the Katō family and raised under that name. His given name was Shūkyo (周挙), his common name Heibei (平兵衛). The surname Kumura, by which he is also known, was a self-chosen name drawn from an ancestral place on the Chita Peninsula. Among his many pen names, the most important are Bōkō (暮雨巷), Ryūmon, and Kyōtai — the last of which he first used in his 1763 anthology Atei-shū (蛙啼集).
He served the Owari Tokugawa domain in Edo as a records official, but resigned at age 28, left Edo, and spent roughly twenty years in wandering before settling in Nagoya's Maetsu district, where he established a hermitage he called Ryūmon. He later resided in the Kuwana-machi quarter of Nagoya, dedicating himself fully to haiku. A building called the Bōkō-an that survives today in Nagoya's Mizuho Ward is a Taishō-era relocation of his original hermitage.
Kyōtai began his haiku study around age 20 under Bajaku (巴雀), and after Bajaku's death continued under Bajaku's son Hakuni (白尼). He became the central figure in Nagoya haiku life, founding the Bōkō school (暮雨巷一門) which flourished from the Hōreki through Meiwa eras. A devoted admirer of Bashō, he particularly revered the anthology Fuyu no Hi (冬の日) and compiled his own Aki no Hi (秋の日, 1772) in conscious homage to it, contributing substantially to the Bashō revival. He also reprinted Kyorai's Kyoraisho and other foundational texts of the Bashō school.
From 1774, Kyōtai made repeated visits to Kyoto, where he associated closely with Buson and his circle. The verse he produced during these Kyoto years is generally regarded as his finest. He became one of the leading voices of the Tenmei-era haiku renaissance (中興俳諧). In 1783 he organized the centennial memorial service for Bashō at Genjūan in Kyoto, and in 1790 he received formal recognition as a haiku master (宗匠) from the Nijō house.
